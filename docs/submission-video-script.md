# Orbital Books video script draft

Rough draft for David to adapt and rehearse. Written with Goldy, November 3, 2026.
Target: five minutes, including clicks and time to read the screen. Screen cues are
not spoken. This is the video script, not the submission README.

## Before recording

- Use a browser that has already accepted the entry page, or show that step briefly.
- Rehearse on the version being submitted. Choose one question that supports a
  clear demonstration without uncovering the best strategy.
- Prepare a save before the decision and a matching save after it, so a recording
  cut can shorten the wait without changing the outcome. Show a genuine result;
  mixed results are welcome. Don't script an improvement the run doesn't produce.
- Show two or three contrasting views of the same question, rather than touring
  every chart. Have a specific Dewey query ready and verify it on that version.
- Keep music below the narration. Leave a little silence for the result and the
  next quarter to register.
- Replace the bracketed narration with the actual question and observed result.

## Opening and why this game

**0:00–0:40. Screen:** Start with a brief view of the station, then show the
business question that will guide the demonstration.

“This is Orbital Books. You run a bookstore in orbit, using data to decide what
to do and then living with the consequences.

“I chose the Bookshop dataset because it fits a business you can imagine running.
I wanted to make exploring it interactive and fun, and I already had game assets
and resources I could draw on.

“The loop is simple: discover a problem, investigate it, make a decision, and see
what happens. Let me show you one trip around that loop.”

## Investigating and collecting evidence

**0:40–1:55. Screen:** Open Investigate. State the question, change a view or
filter, inspect the table behind a chart, and pin a useful view. Ask Dewey one
specific factual question and show its answer. Keep the cursor movements slow.

“The question I'm looking at is [question]. I can start with an overview, narrow
the data, and look at it a few different ways. Here I'm looking at [measure and
dimension]. Switching the view helps me see [an observation visible on screen].

“I can also look at the underlying figures. When I find something I want to keep,
I pin it. I'm building up evidence I can come back to when it's time to decide.

“Dewey lets me ask questions about the data in ordinary language. Here I'll ask
[specific query].

“One deliberate design choice: Dewey helps me investigate, but isn't supposed
to solve the game for me. Its tools give it access to the data I'm allowed to
see. The hidden simulation model and future results are outside that access.

“The power here is having these different ways of looking at the same business,
in one session, and keeping the pieces that matter to me.”

## Making a decision

**1:55–2:35. Screen:** Show the pinned evidence, open Decide, and select a
plausible plan. Briefly show the relevant rules or assumptions. Submit it.

“Now I have to make a call. Based on what I've looked at, I'm going to try
[chosen action]. There are tradeoffs, and I'm not going to give away the best
solutions in this video.

“The original data gives the shop its history. The simulation adds assumptions
about how the business responds, which you can inspect. So this outcome belongs
to the simulated world; it isn't proof that the same action would work in a real
bookstore.

“Let's move forward three months.”

## The result becomes new data

**2:35–3:35. Screen:** Let the result appear. Point to one or two actual outcomes.
Continue into the next quarter and show the played quarter in the available data.

“Here's what happened: [brief description of the actual result].

“This is my favorite part. The decision I just made has become another quarter
of data. I can investigate it, compare it with what came before, and use it when
I make my next decision.

“Whether I solved the previous problem well or badly, there's a new expectation
on the other side. The world moves forward in three-month increments, building
up a history of my decisions and their results.

“That's the appeal of a continuing world to me. There's always another question
to ask. The result is the start of the next investigation.”

## How I built it and what it says about me

**3:35–4:35. Screen:** Stay with the game for the first sentence. Briefly show
Roundhouse and one concrete example from the collaboration log, then return to
the application. Avoid a tour of every agent or pull request.

“I used AI heavily to build this, working with several agents through Roundhouse,
the development environment I'm building around my own ideas of how modern
development should work. I directed the product and design, and used agents for
implementation, testing, and review.

“For example, I didn't want success measured only by profit. The report card also
considers readers, happiness, and culture, and I chose to make the weakest measure
determine the overall score. That's a product choice I wanted the software to
express.

“I went well beyond the suggested 45 minutes. I also reused existing studio
packages and creative resources; those are identified in the submission.

“I wanted you to see the kind of builder you'd be hiring. I care about expressive
interfaces and experiences with a hook: that ‘that was sweet’ moment you remember
after stepping away from the keyboard.”

## Closing

**4:35–5:00. Screen:** End in the next quarter, with the new question or the
accumulated history visible. Show where to find the code and run instructions.

“With more time, I'd make the simulation run continuously, with pause and speed controls, so you could watch the numbers change and intervene during the quarter.”

“The code, run instructions, and supporting data are included in the submission.
What I'd love you to try is this: ask a question, collect some evidence, make a
decision, and come back three months later to find out what you want to ask next.”
